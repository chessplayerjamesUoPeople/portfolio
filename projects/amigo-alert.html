Amigo Alert

An Android app and Azure back end that check in on older adults twice a day and send a text message to an emergency contact when they don't answer.

Role
Sole developer: requirements, design, build, testing, documentation
Timeline
8-week capstone term
Platform
Android (.NET MAUI) + Microsoft Azure
Status
Working prototype

Overview

Why I built it

Between 21% and 43% of older adults in the United States live alone, and about 26.5% of older adults fall each year. Someone who falls, has a stroke, or has a heart attack while alone may be unable to call for help, and every hour without treatment raises the risk of permanent injury or death.

The idea came from close to home. In Cotacachi, Ecuador, an expatriate group once paired retirees to check on each other every day, but that group has since disbanded. A friend of my family later fell, had a stroke, and lay on the floor for about thirty hours before anyone found her. She was left with permanent injuries.

The research mostly points to wearables, thermal sensors, and machine-learning fall detectors. These work well, but they need specialized hardware, cost money many retirees don't have, and come off in the shower, which is exactly where many falls happen. I wanted to see how far a phone the person already owns could go instead.

Approach

Detect silence, not a specific event

Instead of trying to recognize a fall, Amigo Alert notices when a person stops answering. That covers falls, strokes, heart attacks, and loss of consciousness alike.

Escalation runs on the server

The phone can't be trusted to raise the alarm, because a dead battery or lost signal would silence it. The database and the scheduled escalation logic live in Azure, so a device that goes quiet triggers an alert instead of suppressing one.

Consent before any alert

Emergency contacts receive a tokenized link to a consent page and must opt in before the system will ever text them. This satisfies Ecuador's Organic Law on the Protection of Personal Data (LOPDP). None of the reviewed research included a consent step for alert recipients.

How escalation works

  1. 8:00 a.m. & 6:00 p.m. Pending

    Push notification asks the user to check in.

  2. +10 min, no reply Second pending

    A reminder notification is sent.

  3. +20 min, no reply Emergency

    An SMS with the user's location goes to the consented emergency contact.

  4. Any time Okay

    “I'm okay” or “Cancel emergency” resets the status. The emergency button alerts the contact immediately.

Architecture

Nine projects, clear boundaries

The solution is split into API, Data, Services, Shared, Web, WebJob, Tests, and the mobile app with its Android-specific project. The phone never touches the database directly; everything goes through the API.

Services hold the business logic

Escalation, consent, registration, push, SMS, and cleanup each live in their own service class, with an EscalationRules class deciding when to alert. Services reach the database only through repositories, which keeps queries out of business logic and makes the services easy to test.

A WebJob drives the clock

A console app runs as an Azure WebJob on a six-field NCRONTAB schedule (0 * * * * *). Every minute it walks every active user and decides whether to send a check-in, send a reminder, alert the contact, or do nothing.

Code-first data

The schema was built entirely with Entity Framework Core migrations; no hand-written SQL creates tables. Per-table configurations define indexes, keys, and field lengths.

Disciplined branching

Feature branches merged into development through pull requests, then into main for releases, with hotfix branches cut from main. Planner tracked every task.

Designed for older eyes and hands

  • Works in both portrait and landscape on every screen.
  • Status uses symbols as well as color, so it reads in grayscale and for color-blind users.
  • High-contrast text checked against WCAG contrast ratios.
  • Layouts hold up with the system font size at 200%.
  • Large, well-spaced buttons to prevent accidental taps.

Reflection

Trade-offs and next steps

Honest limitations

  • Detection delay. With two check-ins a day, the average wait from an emergency to an alert is about 6.5 hours, and the worst case is 14 hours 20 minutes. Wearables detect events almost immediately; Amigo Alert trades speed for cost, comfort, and coverage.
  • Phone settings matter. In user testing, battery optimization put the app to sleep and a check-in never arrived. Clear setup instructions are needed.
  • SMS can be missed. A plain text message may not feel urgent enough to the contact.
  • Consent withdrawal. Contacts can opt in but can't yet opt out later, which full LOPDP compliance requires.

What comes next

  • A 30-second undo window after the emergency button, so an accidental tap doesn't text anyone.
  • Louder, longer alarm-style reminders with vibration.
  • Adjustable check-in times and more than two check-ins a day.
  • A companion mode for emergency contacts, with alarm-style alerts and in-app consent withdrawal.
  • Using phone motion patterns to trigger an early check-in when the phone hasn't moved for an unusually long time.
  • Moving hosting from about $55/month on Azure PaaS to lower-cost infrastructure.
